# Rule #1: Implement a Password Manager

Small businesses often treat passwords like a shared office supply.

They are written on sticky notes, sent through text messages, saved in spreadsheets, or reused across multiple accounts. Sometimes everyone uses the same login because it feels easier.

> It is easier... until someone leaves, the password is compromised, or nobody knows who accessed the account.

## Start With a Password Manager

A business password manager gives you one secure place to store credentials, control access, and remove users when they no longer need it.

>[!TIP]
>Try a few and see which one you like the best🎉 I recommend [Bitwarden](https://vault.bitwarden.com/#/login), [Keeper](https://keepersecurity.com/vault/#), or [LastPass](https://lastpass.com/login/?lpnorefresh=1)!


This week:

- Choose a business password manager.
- Add your five most critical accounts first.
- Give each employee their own login.
- Stop sending passwords through email or text.
- Use shared vaults instead of sharing one master password.

Do not try to organize every account in one day. Start with email, payroll, banking, accounting software, and Microsoft 365.

## Your Shared Password Is Not a Password Policy

A shared password creates several problems:

- Former employees may still know it.
- MFA may be tied to one person’s phone.
- You cannot tell who accessed the account.
- Changing it disrupts everyone.
- One compromise can expose multiple systems.

Whenever the service allows it, give each employee their own account and only the access they need.

The goal is simple:

> **One password manager, individual user accounts, and no critical passwords living in texts, emails, or sticky notes.**

That one change makes offboarding easier, improves accountability, and removes one of the most common weaknesses in small-business security.
