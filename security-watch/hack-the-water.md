# When Hackers Target the Water Supply: Cybersecurity Is No Longer Just About Data

Most people imagine a cyberattack as stolen passwords, leaked Social Security numbers, or a locked computer. This story shows that cybersecurity failures can affect our infrastructure.

#### [The FBI and EPA issued a joint Public Service Announcement on July 30, 2026](https://www.fbi.gov/investigate/cyber/alerts/2026/malicious-cyber-actors-targeting-water-and-wastewater-sector-internet--facing-programmable-logic-controllers-causing-operational-disruptions) to warn critical infrastructure asset owners and operators that malicious cyber actors (MCAs) are conducting cyber attacks targeting Operational Technology (OT) devices.

> After remotely accessing internet-facing devices, the actors **changed the IP addresses and passwords, resulting in a loss of monitoring and control functionality.**

*Operational technology, or OT, includes the computers and specialized equipment used to monitor and control physical processes. In a water system, programmable logic controllers—or PLCs—may help operate pumps, valves, wells, pressure systems, and treatment equipment.*

## 🏃🏻‍♀️‍➡️Act Now!
> [!IMPORTANT]
> To reduce the risk of compromise, the FBI and EPA recommend removing PLCs from direct internet exposure via secure gateway and firewalls, setting up strong, unique passwords, and utilizing an access control list (ACL) to allow only authorized communication between expected control system devices.

## 🚩Report It!
If you or someone you know has sustained similar OT outages, please contact your local FBI field office and file a complaint with the [Internet Crime Complaint Center](https://www.ic3.gov) at ic3.gov. Additionally, you can contact CISA via CISA’s 24/7 Operations Center at contact@cisa.dhs.gov or 1-844-Say-CISA (1-844-729-2472). Be sure to include as much information as possible:
- Model numbers, serial numbers, and IP addresses of PLCs connected to the network
- Unusual IP addresses observed on networks connected to PLCs

## ⚠️Current Risks
Some affected systems experienced pressure loss or flooding. Losing pressure can create a public-health concern because it may allow untreated groundwater to enter damaged or leaking pipes.

The severity depended on what the compromised controller was responsible for. An attacker interfering with a monitoring device might temporarily blind operators to what was happening. Compromising a controller that directly operates pumps or other equipment could cause a more serious disruption.

The ability to switch to manual operation became an important safeguard. The FBI and EPA recommend that utilities regularly test manual controls, backups, disaster-recovery plans, and other systems needed to continue operating during a cyber incident.
