# One Stolen Password Should Not Ruin Your Life

A stolen password should create one problem.

Password reuse can turn it into ten.

When an email address and password are exposed, criminals may try the same combination on other websites.

They are not necessarily sitting at a keyboard guessing where you shop or bank. Automated tools can test stolen login combinations across many services very quickly.

If you reused that password, one compromised account may provide access to:

- Your email
- Social media
- Shopping accounts
- Cloud storage
- Financial information
- Saved payment methods
- Personal messages
- Photos and documents

This is why every important account needs its own password.

## Protect the Accounts That Control Other Accounts

Some accounts have more power than others.

Your primary email account is especially important because it may receive password-reset links for nearly everything else you use.

Your Apple, Google, or Microsoft account may also control:

- Device backups
- Stored photos
- Saved passwords
- App purchases
- Location information
- Cloud files
- Other connected accounts

Your phone carrier account matters because your phone number may be used for verification codes and account recovery.

Protect these accounts first.

## Find the Passwords You Reused

Many password managers can identify:

- Reused passwords
- Weak passwords
- Old passwords
- Passwords that may have appeared in known breaches

Start with the reused passwords attached to your most important accounts.

Do not create another variation such as adding a number, symbol, or the website’s name. Generate a completely different password for each account.

## What to Do When a Password May Be Stolen

1. Change the password on the affected account.
2. Change it everywhere else you reused it.
3. Start with your primary email account.
4. Turn on multifactor authentication.
5. Review recent sign-ins and connected devices.
6. Remove unfamiliar recovery email addresses or phone numbers.
7. Sign out other sessions when the service provides that option.
8. Watch for unexpected password-reset messages and verification codes.

Changing only the first account does not solve the problem when the same password exists elsewhere.

## One Simple Goal

> [!TIP]
> One password should unlock one account; nothing more.

Using a different password for every account limits the damage.

One company may be breached. One password may be stolen. One login may be phished.

That incident should not automatically hand over the rest of your digital life.
